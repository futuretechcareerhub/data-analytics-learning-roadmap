Can You Solve a Business Problem With Data? A Practical Guide to Analytics Training in India

Learning Excel, SQL or Power BI is useful, but analytics becomes valuable when you can use those tools to answer a real business question. A practical way to evaluate your skills is to take a messy business problem, investigate the evidence and explain what a team should do next.

This guide walks through a fictional retail example and shows what to practise when comparing options for data analytics training in India.

The business problem: sales are growing, but profit is falling

Imagine an online retailer reports that monthly sales increased by 12%, yet its total profit declined. The leadership team wants to know why before changing prices or increasing the marketing budget. “Sales are up” is not enough to explain what is happening.

Start by defining the question clearly: which products, channels, regions or costs explain the change in profit? Agree on the period to compare, confirm the profit definition and check that both months use the same rules.

Step 1: Use Excel to inspect and clean the data

  • Check for blank order IDs, missing product categories, duplicate rows and inconsistent date formats.
  • Confirm that revenue, discounts, returns, shipping costs and profit use consistent units.
  • Build a pivot table to compare sales, profit and profit margin by product category and region.
  • Flag categories where sales increased but profit margin fell sharply.

Keep a short record of any cleaning decisions. If duplicates or missing values change the totals, explain how you handled them instead of silently removing records.

Step 2: Use SQL to test the pattern

After validating the source data, SQL can aggregate transactions and make the investigation repeatable. For example, a query grouped by category can compare revenue and profit across periods:

SELECT
  category,
  SUM(revenue) AS total_revenue,
  SUM(profit) AS total_profit,
  SUM(profit) / NULLIF(SUM(revenue), 0) AS profit_margin
FROM sales
GROUP BY category
ORDER BY total_profit ASC;

This illustrative query assumes a table with category, revenue and profit columns. In a real project, filter to the intended date range and compare equivalent periods. Check how returns, cancelled orders and null values are represented before trusting the results.

Step 3: Investigate causes instead of guessing

Use the results to create testable hypotheses. Perhaps discount-heavy orders grew, returns increased in one category, shipping costs rose in a region, or sales shifted toward lower-margin products. Compare each hypothesis with the available evidence; do not present a possible cause as a fact until you have tested it.

Useful follow-up checks include:

  • Compare profit margin before and after discounts.
  • Review returns and cancellations by category.
  • Compare shipping cost per order across regions.
  • Separate changes in sales volume from changes in unit economics.

Step 4: Build a Power BI dashboard that supports a decision

A good dashboard is not just a collection of charts. It should make the key business question easy to explore. Consider including:

  • KPI cards for revenue, profit and profit margin.
  • A monthly trend showing whether revenue and profit are moving in different directions.
  • A category comparison highlighting high-revenue, low-margin products.
  • Filters for date, region, category and sales channel.

Validate totals against your Excel checks or SQL results. Label the time period, define each KPI and make sure filters do not accidentally change the meaning of the comparison.

Step 5: Turn the analysis into a recommendation

Summarise the finding in plain language: what changed, where the change is concentrated, what evidence supports the explanation and what action should be tested next. For example, if one category's margin dropped alongside heavier discounting, recommend a controlled discount review and measure its impact before rolling it out broadly.

Include a metric and review period for every recommendation. “Improve profitability” is vague; “test a revised discount threshold for two weeks and compare profit per order with the prior period” is measurable.

What to look for in analytics training in India

When reviewing a course, look beyond a list of tools. Check whether the learning experience gives you opportunities to:

  • Clean imperfect datasets and document assumptions.
  • Write SQL queries with grouping, joins, filters and clear calculations.
  • Build dashboards around business questions, not just visuals.
  • Explain findings to a non-technical audience.
  • Complete a portfolio project with a problem statement, method, results and recommendations.
  • Receive feedback on both technical accuracy and communication.

Ask how projects are assessed and whether you can explain each decision in your own work. A project you understand deeply is more useful in an interview than a polished dashboard whose calculations you cannot defend.

A simple portfolio checklist

  • Write a clear business question and define the KPIs.
  • Describe the dataset and document cleaning steps.
  • Include reproducible SQL queries or spreadsheet calculations.
  • Show a dashboard with readable labels and relevant filters.
  • Summarise two or three evidence-backed insights.
  • Recommend next steps and explain how success would be measured.

Build practical skills step by step

You do not need to solve every business question in one sitting. Begin with a small dataset, validate the numbers, test one hypothesis at a time and explain what the evidence can—and cannot—tell you. This process helps connect Excel, SQL and Power BI into a coherent analytical workflow.
