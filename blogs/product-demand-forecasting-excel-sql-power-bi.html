What Should a Business Stock Next Month? Using Data to Forecast Product Demand

A retailer has a familiar problem: some products sell out before the month ends, while others sit in the stockroom for weeks. Ordering too much ties up cash and storage space. Ordering too little can mean missed sales and disappointed customers. A useful demand forecast helps the business estimate what customers may buy next and plan inventory more carefully.

In this practical case study, we will explore how an analyst can use historical sales data, Excel, SQL and Power BI to understand product demand. The goal is not to predict the future perfectly. It is to build a reasoned estimate, make assumptions visible and help the business prepare for different demand scenarios.

Case study note: The business scenario and examples are illustrative. A forecast is an estimate, not a guarantee; actual demand can change because of promotions, prices, supply issues, competition and unexpected events.

Why product demand forecasting matters

Demand forecasting supports decisions across purchasing, warehousing, staffing and cash flow. If a business expects higher demand for a product, it may need to place an order earlier. If demand is slowing, it may avoid unnecessary replenishment or investigate whether the product range needs attention.

Start with specific business questions:

  • Which products have rising, falling or stable demand?
  • How does demand change by week, month or season?
  • Which products frequently run out of stock?
  • Are sales spikes linked to promotions or one-time events?
  • How much stock should be available to cover expected demand and supplier lead time?

Step 1: Understand the data needed

A useful starting dataset may contain order date, product ID, quantity sold, selling price, discount, promotion flag, store or region, and stock availability. Supplier lead time and inventory snapshots can improve replenishment decisions if they are available.

Before analysing, clarify what a sales record represents and how returns, cancelled orders, bundles and out-of-stock days are recorded. Sales are not always the same as true customer demand: when an item is unavailable, recorded sales may be lower than the number of customers who wanted to buy it.

  • Use a consistent time period and product identifier.
  • Check missing dates, duplicate order lines and unusual quantities.
  • Separate returns and cancellations according to agreed business rules.
  • Mark promotion periods and major one-off events where possible.
  • Identify products with short histories or frequent stockouts that need extra caution.

Step 2: Explore demand patterns in Excel

Excel is useful for an initial view of historical sales. Convert the data into a table, check date and quantity formats, and use a PivotTable to summarise units sold by product and month. A line chart can make recurring seasonal patterns, sudden spikes and longer-term changes easier to see.

For a simple baseline, calculate the average monthly units sold over the last three comparable months. If a product sold 90, 105 and 99 units in those months, the simple average is 98 units. This is an illustrative baseline, not a sophisticated forecast. It may be unsuitable when the product has a strong seasonal pattern, a recent trend or a promotion-driven spike.

Compare different windows—such as the latest three months and latest six months—and ask whether the selected period reflects current trading conditions. Avoid treating a one-off sale as normal recurring demand.

Step 3: Use SQL to summarise product sales

SQL helps combine order records and create repeatable summaries. The example below aggregates completed sales by product and calendar month. Date functions differ between database systems, so adapt the syntax to your platform.

SELECT
  product_id,
  DATE_TRUNC('month', order_date) AS sales_month,
  SUM(quantity) AS units_sold,
  SUM(quantity * unit_price) AS sales_value
FROM order_items
WHERE order_status = 'Completed'
GROUP BY
  product_id,
  DATE_TRUNC('month', order_date)
ORDER BY product_id, sales_month;

For a simple moving-average baseline, you can use a window function over the monthly summary. First make sure there is one row for every product-month, including months with zero recorded sales where appropriate; otherwise, the “previous three rows” may not represent three consecutive months.

SELECT
  product_id,
  sales_month,
  units_sold,
  AVG(units_sold) OVER (
    PARTITION BY product_id
    ORDER BY sales_month
    ROWS BETWEEN 3 PRECEDING AND 1 PRECEDING
  ) AS prior_3_month_average
FROM monthly_product_sales;

This produces a baseline from the three preceding rows and excludes the current month. It does not account for seasonality, promotions or stockouts. Treat it as a starting point to compare against more suitable forecasting methods.

Step 4: Consider trend, seasonality and business events

Historical patterns can be informative, but the analyst needs to understand what caused them. Demand may rise during festivals, holidays or weather changes. A discount campaign can create a temporary spike, while a supplier delay can make sales look lower because stock was unavailable.

Separate the key influences:

  • Trend: is demand gradually increasing or decreasing over time?
  • Seasonality: does a pattern repeat at a predictable time of year or week?
  • Promotions: did a discount or campaign temporarily change purchase behaviour?
  • Availability: were products out of stock during periods of recorded low sales?
  • Product lifecycle: is the item new, mature, being replaced or discontinued?

Do not build a forecast from sales history alone when important context is missing. If the business cannot provide promotion or stock availability data, document that limitation in the analysis.

Step 5: Build a Power BI demand dashboard

A Power BI report can help purchasing and category teams explore product trends without reading raw transaction files. Consider these sections:

Demand overview

  • KPI cards for units sold, sales value and number of active products.
  • A monthly demand trend for the selected product or category.
  • A product ranking by units sold over the chosen period.

Forecast and comparison

  • Historical monthly units alongside the selected baseline estimate.
  • A comparison of recent average demand with the same period in the previous year, where data supports it.
  • Filters for product, category, store, region and date range.

Inventory planning context

  • Current stock and supplier lead time, if available.
  • Products below a defined replenishment threshold.
  • Clear notes showing forecast method, data freshness and known limitations.

Keep historical actuals visually distinct from estimates. Label forecast periods clearly and avoid presenting an uncertain point estimate as a guaranteed quantity.

Step 6: Check whether the forecast is useful

A forecast should be tested against data that was not used to build it. One simple approach is to use earlier months to create an estimate, then compare that estimate with the actual demand in a later month. Repeat this across several periods when enough history is available.

Common error measures include Mean Absolute Error (MAE), which averages the absolute difference between forecast and actual values, and Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), which expresses error as a percentage. MAPE can be misleading or undefined when actual demand is zero or very small, so choose an evaluation measure that suits the product and business context.

Compare the proposed method with a simple baseline, such as last month's demand or a seasonal-naive estimate. A more complex method is not automatically better if it does not improve decisions in practice.

Step 7: Turn the estimate into a stock planning decision

Forecast demand is only one input to replenishment. The business also needs to consider current inventory, outstanding purchase orders, supplier lead time, minimum order quantities, safety stock and service targets.

For example, a product expected to sell 100 units over the next month may need a different order quantity if 40 units are already available and another 30 are due to arrive. The analyst should help reconcile the forecast with inventory and incoming supply rather than simply recommending an order of 100 units.

Present recommendations as scenarios where uncertainty is high: a base estimate, a higher-demand case and a lower-demand case. Agree on who will review the assumptions and when the forecast will be refreshed.

How to present this as a Data Analyst portfolio project

  1. Business problem: explain why the retailer needs better product-level stock planning.
  2. Dataset and assumptions: describe the source, time range, product grain and treatment of returns or stockouts.
  3. Excel exploration: show monthly summaries and demand patterns.
  4. SQL analysis: include product-month aggregation and moving-average queries.
  5. Forecast evaluation: explain the baseline, test period and error measure.
  6. Power BI dashboard: show historical trends, estimates and inventory context.
  7. Recommendation: present a practical replenishment scenario and its limitations.

Use synthetic or public data for a public portfolio. Never publish confidential supplier terms, internal stock levels or customer information. Include a short README so another person can understand your method and reproduce the analysis.

Final takeaway

Product demand forecasting is not about guessing a perfect number. It is about using reliable history, understanding trends and business events, testing a sensible baseline and combining the estimate with inventory and supplier information. Excel supports exploration, SQL creates repeatable summaries and Power BI helps teams review the evidence.
