Data Analytics Case Study: How to Turn Raw Data into Business Insights

A practical end-to-end example of moving from a business question to evidence-based recommendations.

Published: September 25, 2026

Quick answer: A data analytics case study demonstrates how you use data to answer a real business question. Start by defining the decision and success metrics, inspect and clean the data, analyze it with tools such as SQL or Excel, validate the findings, and communicate specific recommendations with assumptions and limitations.

The goal is not simply to produce charts. A useful analysis explains what happened, where and why it may have happened, how confident you are in the finding, and what decision or next investigation the evidence supports.

What Is a Data Analytics Case Study?

A data analytics case study is a structured investigation of a business or operational problem using data. It usually includes the context, questions, data sources, analysis method, findings, recommendations and limitations.

It can be based on a real workplace problem, a public dataset or a clearly labeled simulated business scenario. If you use synthetic data or make assumptions, disclose that clearly rather than presenting the results as actual company performance.

Case Study Scenario: Retail Sales Performance

Imagine a retail business has noticed that revenue is growing but profit is not improving at the same pace. The leadership team wants to understand which product categories, regions and sales channels are contributing to the gap.

For this illustrative case, assume the available dataset contains order date, order ID, product category, region, channel, quantity, sales, discount and cost. These are example fields; a real analysis must first confirm what each column means and how it was collected.

Step 1: Define the Business Problem

Before opening Excel or writing SQL, clarify the decision the analysis should support. “Analyze sales” is too broad. Turn it into focused questions:

  • How have sales, gross profit and order volume changed over time?
  • Which categories and regions contribute most to revenue and profit?
  • Are discounts associated with lower margins in particular segments?
  • Are changes driven by order volume, average order value, product mix or costs?
  • What additional information is needed before recommending an action?

Agree on the time period, business definitions, target audience and expected deliverable. Confirm whether “sales” means booked revenue, net sales after discounts, or another company-defined measure.

Step 2: Identify Metrics and KPIs

MetricIllustrative definitionQuestion it helps answer
Net salesSales after applicable discounts and returns, according to the agreed business definitionHow much revenue was retained?
Gross profitNet sales minus the cost of goods sold, where those fields are availableWhat remains after direct product costs?
Gross marginGross profit divided by net salesWhat share of net sales remains as gross profit?
Order countDistinct valid order identifiersHow many orders were placed?
Average order valueNet sales divided by valid order countWhat is the average net sales amount per order?
Discount rateDiscount amount divided by the appropriate pre-discount sales baseHow much discount is being applied?

Define numerator, denominator, filters, date logic and exclusions for every KPI. For example, an order-level average should not be calculated from line-item rows without first deciding how order totals will be aggregated.

Step 3: Inspect and Clean the Data

Start with a data dictionary and profile the dataset. Check row counts, column types, date ranges, unique identifiers, null values, duplicates, invalid values and unexpected category labels.

  • Check whether one row represents an order, an order line or a product-day record.
  • Identify duplicate records using the correct business key rather than deleting every repeated-looking row.
  • Inspect missing sales, cost, discount and date values; determine whether they mean zero, unknown or not applicable.
  • Check for negative quantities, returns, cancellations and adjustments, and confirm how they should be treated.
  • Standardize category and region labels without merging genuinely different categories.
  • Validate totals against a trusted source or source-system report when available.

Keep a record of cleaning decisions. Do not silently drop rows or replace missing values in ways that change the business meaning.

Step 4: Explore the Data with SQL

SQL helps aggregate large datasets consistently. For example, after confirming that sales and cost are line-level amounts and that the table contains valid order lines, a monthly summary might look like this:

SELECT
  DATE_TRUNC('month', order_date) AS month,
  SUM(net_sales) AS net_sales,
  SUM(net_sales - cost) AS gross_profit,
  COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS orders
FROM cleaned_order_lines
WHERE order_status = 'Completed'
GROUP BY 1
ORDER BY 1;

This is illustrative SQL; date functions, status filters and field names vary by database and data model. If returns, taxes, shipping, or cancellations affect the official KPI definition, incorporate the approved logic before interpreting the output.

Then segment results by category, region and channel. Compare like-for-like periods and account for seasonality or changes in the number of operating days.

Step 5: Analyze Trends and Possible Drivers

Move from a top-level KPI to its components. If gross profit is changing, examine net sales, direct costs, product mix, discounts and order volume. A useful decomposition can help distinguish whether the change is associated with fewer orders, smaller baskets, different products or lower margins.

Use several views:

  • Time trend: monthly or weekly sales and margin, with comparable periods.
  • Category comparison: revenue, gross profit and margin by product category.
  • Regional view: performance by region, while checking differences in store count or market size.
  • Discount analysis: compare discount levels and margin, while noting that correlation does not prove discounts caused the result.
  • Order behavior: order count, average order value and product mix.

Do not assume that the segment with the largest revenue is automatically the most profitable or the segment that needs intervention.

Step 6: Turn Findings into Insights

A finding describes a pattern in the data. An insight explains why the pattern matters in the business context and what should be examined or done next.

Weak statementMore decision-useful statement structure
“Category A has high sales.”“Category A contributes a defined share of net sales; compare its gross profit and margin with the business target before deciding whether to expand it.”
“Discounts are high.”“Discounts are concentrated in specific segments. Validate margin after discounts and test whether the promotion produces incremental sales before changing policy.”
“Region B is down.”“Region B's result changed over the selected period. Break it down by order volume, product mix, store coverage and comparable-period effects to investigate possible drivers.”

These are examples of how to frame analysis, not actual findings from a real retailer. Use measured values and verified comparisons in your own case study.

Step 7: Build a Clear Report or Presentation

Present information in the order a decision-maker needs it:

  1. Executive summary: business question, key verified findings and the decision requested.
  2. KPI snapshot: a small set of clearly defined metrics with period and comparison.
  3. Trend and segment analysis: charts that directly answer the investigation questions.
  4. Recommendations: proposed actions, expected rationale, owner or next step, and how success will be measured.
  5. Limitations: data gaps, assumptions, uncertainty and further analysis needed.

Choose charts to fit the question. A line chart can show trends over time, a sorted bar chart can compare categories, and a scatter plot can help explore relationships. Use labels, units, clear time ranges and accessible colors. Avoid 3D charts, clutter and axes that exaggerate small differences.

Step 8: Make Recommendations Measurable

Recommendations should connect to evidence and include a way to assess their impact. Instead of proposing a broad discount reduction based only on a correlation, suggest validating the relevant segments and, where feasible, running a controlled or phased test.

For each recommendation, document:

  • The finding and evidence supporting it
  • The proposed action and target segment
  • The KPI and baseline to monitor
  • The test period and responsible owner
  • Risks, dependencies and what result would change the recommendation

Where a causal effect matters, observational analysis may not be enough. An experiment or a stronger causal design may be needed to estimate whether an action caused a business outcome.

Tools You Can Use

Choose tools based on the dataset, task and audience. A clear, validated analysis in a spreadsheet can be more useful than a complicated dashboard that does not answer the business question.

How to Present a Case Study in Your Portfolio

Make your project reproducible and easy to review. A useful GitHub or portfolio structure includes:

  • README.md with the business context, questions, dataset source and assumptions
  • Data dictionary and documented cleaning steps
  • SQL queries or analysis notebook
  • Dashboard screenshots or a public demo that contains no sensitive data
  • Summary of findings, recommendations and limitations
  • Instructions to reproduce the analysis, where licensing permits

Never upload confidential employer data, personal information or datasets whose license does not allow redistribution. Use public or synthetic data when building a shareable portfolio project, and label simulated results accurately.

Common Data Analytics Case Study Mistakes

  • Starting with charts before defining the business decision.
  • Using KPIs without agreed definitions or denominators.
  • Ignoring row-level grain, duplicates, returns or missing data.
  • Confusing correlation with causation.
  • Presenting every chart instead of answering the key questions.
  • Making recommendations without a measurable follow-up.
  • Hiding assumptions, data limitations or simulated data.
  • Sharing sensitive data in public repositories or dashboards.

Practice Exercise

Choose a public retail or sales dataset and answer one focused question, such as: “Which product segments contribute to changes in gross margin?” Write down the KPI definitions, inspect the data grain, validate totals, analyze trends and segments, then create a one-page summary with two or three evidence-backed findings and a measurable next step.

Frequently Asked Questions

What should a data analytics case study include?

Include the business problem, data source, KPI definitions, cleaning and analysis method, validated findings, recommendations, limitations and a clear presentation of the results.

Which tools are used for data analytics case studies?

Excel or Sheets, SQL, Power BI or Tableau, and Python are common options. The appropriate mix depends on data size, analysis requirements and the audience.

How do I choose a case study topic?

Choose a specific, answerable business question with an accessible dataset and meaningful outcome. Make sure the data fields support the questions you want to investigate.

How is an insight different from a finding?

A finding describes an observed pattern. An insight connects that pattern to business context and explains why it matters or what should be investigated next.

Can I use synthetic data in my portfolio?

Yes, if you clearly label it as synthetic and do not present its results as real business performance. Explain the assumptions and demonstrate the analysis process.

How do I make recommendations from data?

Connect each recommendation to validated evidence, define the target segment and success metric, disclose limitations, and use a test or further analysis when causal evidence is required.
