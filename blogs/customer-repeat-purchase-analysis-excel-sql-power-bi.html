Why Are Some Customers Buying Again While Others Never Return? A Repeat Purchase Analysis Using Excel, SQL & Power BI

Investigate customer purchase behaviour, distinguish repeat buyers from one-time customers, and turn the patterns into practical retention ideas.

Published: November 28, 2026 · Business analytics case study

A business may attract many new customers but still struggle to grow steadily if most people never make a second purchase. Looking only at total orders or revenue can hide this pattern. A practical analyst investigates who returns, when they return, what they buy again and which customer groups have different repeat-purchase behaviour.

Business question: Are customers returning to buy again—and what differences can we observe between repeat buyers and customers who purchased only once?

1. Define the analysis before opening the tools

First, agree on what counts as a repeat purchase. For this example, a customer is a repeat buyer if they have placed at least two distinct completed orders within the analysis period. The analyst should exclude cancelled orders and clarify how refunds or partial returns are handled.

  • How many unique customers purchased during the period?
  • What percentage placed more than one order?
  • How long does it typically take customers to place their next order?
  • Do repeat purchase rates vary by product category, acquisition channel or customer cohort?
  • Are repeat buyers contributing more revenue, and how does order value compare?

2. Prepare the customer and order data in Excel

Start with order-level data containing customer IDs, order IDs, order dates, order status, product category, quantity, sales and channel. If customer information is stored separately, note the relationship between the tables.

Check unique identifiers: confirm order IDs and customer IDs are consistent and that the order table’s grain is understood.
Clean the fields: standardise dates, remove or flag duplicate records, check missing customer IDs and correct inconsistent labels.
Filter order status: decide whether cancelled, pending, refunded or returned orders should be included and document the rule.
Validate counts: compare the number of records, orders and customers before and after cleaning; verify totals.

Excel can support this work through filters, formulas, conditional formatting, PivotTables and simple charts. Avoid assigning repeat status using row counts if duplicate order lines might inflate the number of orders; count distinct order IDs instead.

3. Use SQL to classify customers

SQL can aggregate transactions into customer-level metrics and classify each customer based on their number of completed orders. The example below assumes an orders table with one row per order. If the source contains order lines, aggregate distinct order IDs first.

WITH customer_orders AS (
  SELECT
    customer_id,
    COUNT(DISTINCT order_id) AS order_count,
    MIN(order_date) AS first_order_date,
    MAX(order_date) AS latest_order_date,
    SUM(order_amount) AS total_spend
  FROM orders
  WHERE order_status = 'Completed'
  GROUP BY customer_id
)
SELECT
  customer_id,
  order_count,
  total_spend,
  CASE
    WHEN order_count >= 2 THEN 'Repeat buyer'
    ELSE 'One-time buyer'
  END AS purchase_segment,
  first_order_date,
  latest_order_date
FROM customer_orders;

This creates a customer-level view. The exact filters, date window and revenue definition should match the business rules agreed upon for the project.

4. Calculate repeat-purchase metrics

Once customers have been classified, create a small set of measures that stakeholders can understand and compare consistently.

MetricDefinitionWhy it matters
Unique customersCount of distinct customers with eligible purchasesShows the customer base included in the analysis
Repeat buyersCustomers with two or more eligible ordersQuantifies returning customers
Repeat purchase rateRepeat buyers ÷ unique customers × 100Shows the share of customers who purchased again during the selected period
Orders per customerTotal eligible orders ÷ unique customersIndicates average purchase frequency
Average order valueEligible revenue ÷ eligible ordersCompares basket size across segments
Time to second orderDays between first and second eligible orders for customers with a second orderHelps understand the repeat-purchase timing

These metrics are period-dependent. A customer who joined near the end of the date range has had less time to return than someone who joined earlier, so cohort comparisons can provide a fairer view.

5. Compare customer segments and cohorts

Aggregate metrics can hide meaningful differences. Break the analysis down carefully to explore possible patterns:

  • Category: compare repeat behaviour across categories; consumable or replenishment products may have different purchase cycles than durable goods.
  • Acquisition channel: check whether customers from different channels return at different rates, while considering spend and campaign context.
  • Customer cohort: group customers by first-purchase month and compare repeat behaviour over similar time windows.
  • Purchase timing: examine days between orders and identify common return windows.
  • Order value: compare average spend and total contribution across one-time and repeat buyers.

Use these breakdowns to generate hypotheses—not to claim that a channel, discount or category caused customers to return. Validate interpretations with additional evidence or a controlled test where appropriate.

6. Build a Power BI dashboard

A compact dashboard could include these sections:

  • Summary cards: unique customers, repeat buyers, repeat purchase rate, orders per customer.
  • Customer split: repeat vs one-time buyers, with clear counts and percentages.
  • Segment comparison: repeat rate by category or channel.
  • Cohort view: how customer groups return over time after their first purchase.
  • Timing: distribution of days to second order.
  • Filters: date range, category, channel and region where available.
Dashboard quality check: verify every KPI against a SQL result or a validated spreadsheet, ensure filters behave as expected, and explain the date window and customer definition prominently.

7. Translate findings into actions

Possible pattern foundHypothesis to explorePotential next step
Repeat rate is lower for one categoryProduct fit, quality, replenishment cycle or customer expectations may differReview feedback and category-specific purchase timing
Many first-time buyers do not return within the expected windowOnboarding, follow-up or product experience may need attentionTest a helpful follow-up message or post-purchase experience
One channel has a strong repeat rateChannel audience or campaign messaging could be attracting customers with different needsCompare acquisition cost and customer value, not repeat rate alone
Repeat buyers have higher total spendMore orders may contribute to higher lifetime revenueStudy margin, order value and customer-level contribution too

Recommendations should be prioritised by potential impact, cost, feasibility and the strength of the evidence. A suggested retention campaign should have a measurable test plan—for example, define the eligible segment, treatment, comparison group and success metric.

8. Turn the work into a portfolio project

Document the project so a reviewer can see how you think, not just the finished dashboard:

  1. Business problem and repeat-purchase definition.
  2. Dataset description, data cleaning decisions and validation checks.
  3. SQL queries for customer classification and metric calculations.
  4. Dashboard screenshots and a short explanation of each visual.
  5. Key findings, limitations and recommendations.
  6. Suggested experiment or follow-up analysis.

Skills this case study practises

  • Excel data cleaning, validation, formulas and PivotTables.
  • SQL aggregation, distinct counting, CTEs, date logic and customer-level analysis.
  • Power BI KPI design, visualisation, filters and cohort-style reporting.
  • Business interpretation, analytical reasoning and insight communication.
  • Project documentation and portfolio presentation.

Conclusion

Repeat-purchase analysis helps businesses look beyond acquisition and understand customer behaviour after the first transaction. A learner can practise a full analytics workflow by defining repeat buyers carefully, cleaning order data, calculating metrics, comparing customer segments and communicating evidence-based ideas for improving the customer experience.
