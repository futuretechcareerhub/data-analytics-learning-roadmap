The Average Looks Fine, but Customers Are Leaving: A Data Analytics Investigation

Imagine this: a subscription business reports stable average monthly revenue and a respectable overall customer satisfaction score. Yet new customer complaints are rising, repeat purchases are slowing, and the sales team says renewals feel harder than last quarter. The headline averages look fine—but customers are quietly leaving.

This is a useful real-world challenge for anyone learning Analytics Training in India: business averages can hide important changes in specific customer groups. A data analyst needs to go beyond a single KPI, examine behaviour over time, and explain which customers are leaving, when the change began, and what the business can test next.

Why healthy averages can hide a retention problem

An average combines many different experiences into one number. Long-term customers may continue buying regularly while recently acquired customers stop after their first order. A large, loyal segment can keep the overall retention rate looking steady even as a smaller but strategically important group deteriorates.

Other effects can hide the pattern too:

  • Customer mix changes: a larger share of established customers can mask weaker results among new customers.
  • Different observation windows: customers who joined last week have had less time to return than customers who joined six months ago.
  • Seasonality: a normal seasonal dip can be confused with a new retention issue—or conceal one.
  • Survivorship bias: reports about active customers naturally exclude many who already left.
  • Metric definitions: “active,” “retained,” and “churned” may mean different things across teams.
Analyst's principle: an average is a starting point, not a diagnosis. Break the metric into meaningful segments and compare equivalent time periods before recommending action.

Step 1: Define the business question and retention metric

Start with a precise question: Are customers acquired in recent months returning at a lower rate than earlier customer groups? This is more useful than simply asking why churn increased, because it identifies a measurable pattern to investigate.

Agree on the definitions before calculating anything. For an online retailer, a returning customer might be someone who makes another purchase within 30 days. For a subscription service, retention may mean the subscription remains active at the end of a billing period. The appropriate definition depends on the business model.

A basic period retention formula is:

Retention rate = Customers from the starting group still active at period end
                 ÷ Customers in the starting group × 100

Use a consistent starting group and observation window. Do not compare a fully observed 30-day period with a group that has only been observed for ten days.

Step 2: Check the data before trusting the result

Before building a dashboard, verify that the source data can answer the question. In Excel, inspect duplicate customer IDs, blank dates, inconsistent status labels, impossible timestamps, and orders that have been cancelled or refunded. Check whether customer IDs remain consistent across exports.

Useful validation questions include:

  • Does each row represent a customer, an order, or a customer-period record?
  • Are order dates and customer sign-up dates stored as actual dates?
  • Are test accounts, duplicate transactions, and cancelled orders handled consistently?
  • Do customer totals reconcile with the trusted source report?
  • Are all cohorts measured for the same number of days?

Document exclusions and assumptions. A clean-looking chart is not evidence of trustworthy data unless the underlying logic has been checked.

Step 3: Use SQL to build customer cohorts

A cohort groups customers by a shared starting event, such as the month of their first purchase. Instead of blending all customers together, compare each group across its first month, second month, third month, and so on. This helps reveal whether newer cohorts are returning less often than older ones.

A simplified SQL pattern for monthly first-purchase cohorts might look like this. Adapt table and column names, date functions, and database syntax to your environment.

WITH first_purchase AS (
  SELECT
    customer_id,
    MIN(order_date) AS first_order_date
  FROM orders
  WHERE order_status = 'completed'
  GROUP BY customer_id
),
customer_activity AS (
  SELECT DISTINCT
    o.customer_id,
    DATE_TRUNC('month', f.first_order_date) AS cohort_month,
    DATE_TRUNC('month', o.order_date) AS activity_month
  FROM orders o
  JOIN first_purchase f
    ON o.customer_id = f.customer_id
  WHERE o.order_status = 'completed'
)
SELECT
  cohort_month,
  activity_month,
  COUNT(DISTINCT customer_id) AS active_customers
FROM customer_activity
GROUP BY cohort_month, activity_month
ORDER BY cohort_month, activity_month;

This query produces active-customer counts by cohort and activity month. To calculate retention percentages, also calculate each cohort's starting customer count and divide the active count in each later period by that baseline. Confirm that the cohort-month period is numbered consistently and that the first period is treated as the baseline.

SQL is especially helpful when the dataset is too large for manual inspection, when the same metric must be refreshed regularly, or when customer activity is stored across multiple tables.

These illustrative figures show a decline in repeat purchasing among newer cohorts. The next step is not to claim that a particular campaign caused the decline. Instead, investigate what changed around the relevant acquisition periods: traffic source, offer type, product availability, delivery times, onboarding, pricing, or customer support.

Segment the cohorts by channel, region, product category, first-order discount, or customer type—provided the sample sizes are large enough and the comparison is fair. If the decline is concentrated in one segment, that gives the business a more focused question to test.

Step 5: Turn the findings into a Power BI retention dashboard

A useful dashboard helps a stakeholder move from the overall metric to the segment that needs attention. Keep it focused on a few decision-supporting visuals rather than filling the page with charts.

  • KPI cards: show retention, repeat-purchase rate, churn, and the period-over-period change, with definitions visible.
  • Cohort heatmap: place cohort month on one axis and months since acquisition on the other; use colour to reveal weak retention periods.
  • Trend line: compare retention across equivalent observation windows.
  • Segment comparison: allow filtering by acquisition channel, region, or product group.
  • Context panel: record sample size, data freshness, metric definition, and known limitations.

In Power BI, check that slicers affect the intended visuals, totals remain consistent under filters, and users can distinguish percentages from customer counts. Avoid ranking tiny segments as if their results were as reliable as those of large groups.

Step 6: Recommend a test, not a guess

Once the likely problem area is identified, propose an action that can be evaluated. For example, if first-time buyers from one acquisition channel rarely return, the team could test a more relevant follow-up message, improve product guidance, or investigate fulfilment delays for that group.

Define the success metric and comparison method before launch. Where practical, use a controlled experiment or a suitable comparison group. Track both the intended outcome and guardrails such as refunds, complaints, unsubscribe rates, and margin. A retention improvement that comes only from heavy discounts may not be commercially healthy.

Present the result in a simple structure: what changed, who is affected, what evidence supports the explanation, what action should be tested, and how success will be measured. Clearly label hypotheses that still need validation.

Build this into a portfolio project

If you are learning analytics, turn this scenario into a small, well-documented project. Use a public or synthetic dataset and include:

  1. A one-paragraph business problem and clear definition of retention.
  2. A data dictionary and notes on cleaning decisions.
  3. Excel checks for duplicates, missing values, and basic summaries.
  4. SQL queries for first-purchase cohorts and repeat activity.
  5. A Power BI cohort heatmap with a small set of supporting KPIs.
  6. Three evidence-based findings and one testable recommendation.
  7. A limitations section explaining what the data cannot prove.

A strong portfolio project does not need dramatic conclusions. It needs reproducible calculations, clear reasoning, honest limitations, and recommendations that follow from the evidence.
