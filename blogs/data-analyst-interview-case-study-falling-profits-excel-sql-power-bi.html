Data Analyst Interview Case Study: How to Analyze Falling Profits Using Excel, SQL & Power BI

Published: October 14, 2026 · Practical interview case study

Imagine a retail company reports that revenue is almost unchanged this month, but profit has fallen sharply. The hiring manager asks: “How would you investigate this?” A strong data analyst does not jump straight to a conclusion. They clarify the metric, validate the data, break profit into drivers, test segments and explain what the evidence does—and does not—show.

This fictional case study gives you a repeatable framework for an interview or portfolio project using Excel, SQL and Power BI. All figures below are illustrative, not results from a real company.

Business question: Why did monthly profit decline, which products or channels contributed most, and what should the business investigate next?

1. Clarify the business metric first

Start by asking how the company defines profit. It could mean gross profit, contribution margin or operating profit. For this exercise, use gross profit:

Gross profit = net sales − cost of goods sold (COGS)

Gross margin % = gross profit ÷ net sales × 100

Confirm whether net sales exclude discounts, cancellations and returns, and whether COGS includes the costs relevant to the chosen definition. Agree on the reporting timezone, currency and two complete comparison periods. If these definitions change between months, the comparison may be misleading.

2. Validate the dataset before analysing it

Assume the dataset contains order date, order ID, product, category, region, channel, quantity, unit selling price, discount and unit cost. Before calculating anything:

  • Check missing dates, product names, quantities, selling prices and unit costs.
  • Investigate duplicate order-line IDs rather than deleting them blindly.
  • Confirm how returns, cancelled orders and refunded discounts are represented.
  • Check for negative quantities, unusually high discounts and unexpected cost values.
  • Make sure both months are complete and the data refresh succeeded.

In Excel, use filters, conditional formatting, pivot tables and duplicate checks to identify suspicious rows. Record how each exception was handled so the analysis can be reproduced. For more validation ideas, read When Numbers Lie: A Practical Guide to Data Quality for Analysts.

3. Calculate the key profit metrics

Consider this simplified example:

MetricPrevious monthCurrent monthChange
Net sales₹10,00,000₹10,10,000+1.0%
COGS₹6,00,000₹6,60,000+10.0%
Gross profit₹4,00,000₹3,50,000−12.5%
Gross margin40.0%34.7%−5.3 percentage points

Here, sales rose slightly while gross profit fell. That points the investigation toward costs, discounts or product mix—but it does not yet prove which factor caused the change. Always calculate percentage-point changes separately from percentage changes.

4. Use SQL to compare months and product segments

Suppose a cleaned table named sales_lines has one row per order line and columns for order_date, order_line_id, status, net_sales, cogs, category and channel. Adapt date syntax to your database and verify that amounts are stored at the stated row grain.

SELECT
  CASE
    WHEN order_date >= DATE '2026-08-01'
     AND order_date <  DATE '2026-09-01' THEN 'previous_month'
    WHEN order_date >= DATE '2026-09-01'
     AND order_date <  DATE '2026-10-01' THEN 'current_month'
  END AS period,
  category,
  channel,
  SUM(net_sales) AS net_sales,
  SUM(cogs) AS cogs,
  SUM(net_sales - cogs) AS gross_profit,
  100.0 * SUM(net_sales - cogs)
    / NULLIF(SUM(net_sales), 0) AS gross_margin_pct
FROM sales_lines
WHERE status = 'completed'
  AND order_date >= DATE '2026-08-01'
  AND order_date <  DATE '2026-10-01'
GROUP BY period, category, channel
ORDER BY period, category, channel;

This is a template, not a universal query. If discounts or returns are stored separately, incorporate them according to the documented metric definition. Avoid summing an order-level amount repeated across multiple line items, as this can inflate totals.

5. Investigate the likely profit drivers

Use a consistent breakdown by category, product, region and sales channel. Start with absolute profit change so you can see which segments explain the largest part of the decline; then review margin percentage to understand efficiency.

  • Higher unit costs: compare supplier prices, freight or cost updates by product and date.
  • Deeper discounts: compare discount rates and promotion calendars by channel.
  • Product mix shift: check whether more low-margin products made up the sales mix.
  • Returns and refunds: verify whether return rates or refund amounts changed.
  • Data or accounting changes: inspect pipeline logs, mapping changes and cost-allocation rules.

Do not assume that the segment with the lowest margin caused the decline. Quantify each segment's contribution to the total change and check whether the pattern persists across comparable periods.

6. Build a focused Power BI dashboard

A useful interview dashboard should answer the business question without overwhelming the reader. Consider these visuals:

  • KPI cards for net sales, gross profit, gross margin and month-over-month change.
  • A monthly trend line for sales, COGS and gross profit.
  • A ranked bar chart of profit change by product category.
  • A matrix with category, channel, sales, profit and margin.
  • Slicers for date, region, category and channel.

Keep units and comparison periods visible. Test slicers, reconcile totals against SQL, and avoid presenting incomplete dates as complete periods. See Data Analytics Dashboard Design and seven dashboard data-trust tests for additional checks.

7. Turn findings into a cautious recommendation

A good case-study answer separates observations from hypotheses. Use this structure:

  1. Finding: state how profit and margin changed over comparable periods.
  2. Driver: identify the categories or channels contributing most to the absolute profit decline.
  3. Evidence: name the checks that support the explanation, such as cost records or promotion history.
  4. Next action: recommend a focused investigation or test with a measurable outcome.
  5. Limitation: disclose missing data or factors that have not yet been ruled out.

For the illustrative figures, a careful summary might be: “Net sales increased 1%, while gross profit fell 12.5% and gross margin declined from 40.0% to 34.7%. The next step is to quantify the category-level profit change and validate unit costs, discounts and product mix before assigning a cause.”

Practise this as a portfolio project

  • Create a small synthetic sales dataset with at least two complete months.
  • Document the profit definition and data-quality rules.
  • Use Excel to inspect data and calculate baseline metrics.
  • Use SQL to aggregate profit and margin by month, category and channel.
  • Build a Power BI dashboard and validate its totals against SQL.
  • Write a one-page decision brief with evidence, recommendations and limitations.

Include your SQL, metric definitions, dashboard screenshots and a short explanation of what you would investigate next. A portfolio project is stronger when a reviewer can follow your reasoning, not just see a finished visual.
